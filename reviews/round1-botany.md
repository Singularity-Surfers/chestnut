# Round 1 botanical review — old-growth chestnut cove, mid-October

Reviewer role: field botanist / forest ecologist (southern Appalachian flora). Judged from images only, no source code read.

Material examined: all leaf, fern, twig and bark sheets (`shots/sheets1`); all twelve whole-scene views (`shots/set3`); all 36 tree/shrub portraits and all 26 ground portraits in `shots/portraits2` (47 at full size, 15 as reduced tiles or centre crops); the Richards painting, two historical chestnut photographs and eight species reference photos. The contact sheets `_trees.jpg` and `_ground.jpg` named in the task do not exist in the folder. The fern sheet is cut off at the bottom, so the bases of the bracken and New York fern fronds could not be checked.

Short version: the painted leaf sprites are the strongest part of the project and most would be recognised by a botanist on sight. The forest they are hung on is not yet convincing. Trunks are flared cones, crowns are small tufts, hemlocks are blue, the herb layer is mostly unidentifiable in the world, several communities (streamside rhododendron thicket, dry-ridge heath) are missing, and old-growth structure (snags, logs, tip-up mounds, closed heavy-limbed canopy) is largely absent.

## 3. Notes

### Portraits that fail to show their subject (a finding in itself)

- `tree_whiteoak`, `tree_tulip`, `tree_tulip_giant`, `tree_buckeye`, `tree_ash` all show the same stream-and-trail view as `set3/october.png`. The camera did not go to the tree.
- `tree_chestnut` and `tree_chestnut_giant` are the same upward view; `tree_hemlock` and `tree_hemlock_giant` are the same view of a sapling.
- `tree_redoak` and `tree_ybirch` are blocked by a huge foreground bole.
- `tree_chestnutoak`, `tree_redmaple`, `tree_beech`, `tree_frasermagnolia`, `tree_cucumbertree`: the plant at the centre does not match the named species.
- Ground: galax, wintergreen, bottlebrush grass, cucumber-root, striped wintergreen, chestnut seedling, aster, foamflower and panic grass are not identifiable at the image centre.
- Consequence: I could not confirm the in-world look of about a third of the species. Scores for those rest on sprites alone.

### Old-growth structure

- **Trunks.** Almost every large tree is a smooth cone that flares into a wide skirt and meets the ground at a hard edge (`bark.png`, `ridge.png`, `trunk.png`, `stream.png`). It looks like a tent or a swamp cypress. The historical photos in the brief show columnar boles with a modest swell broken into separate mossy root buttresses.
- **Taper.** `trunk.png` shows the bole narrowing to a point with four limbs leaving at one height in a cross. Old chestnuts and tulip poplars hold their thickness for 15–20 m and then divide into a few huge ascending limbs at staggered heights.
- **Crowns.** From above (`canopy.png`) crowns are narrow tufts with trunks and ground visible between them. An old-growth cove canopy is closed, with crowns 12–20 m wide.
- **Size mix.** Good: giants, mid-sized trees, poles and saplings are all present.
- **Dead wood.** I saw no standing snag anywhere, one large log (the gap), and two mossy logs. No tip-up mound, no pit, no root plate on the fallen tree. This is the biggest gap between "big-tree forest" and "old growth".
- **Gap.** `gap.png` is not visibly different from the closed forest. A real gap has a root plate and pit, a crown wreck, and a thicket of saplings and light-demanding herbs.
- **Layering.** Canopy, understory tree, shrub and herb layers all exist. The understory trees are mostly dense balls on poles, which flattens the layering.
- **Moss.** The moss at tree bases is a uniform green gradient band. It should be patchy, heavier on root buttresses and the north side. No lichen on bark.

### Community composition

- The view from above matches the brief's "immense golden canopy punctuated by islands of scarlet and orange", with dark hemlock accents. That part works.
- At eye level hemlock appears in almost every frame, including upland slopes. It looks nearer 15–20% of stems than 8%.
- I cannot verify 35% chestnut from the images, because trunks are hard to tell apart in the world and the chestnut portraits failed. Chestnut should be unmistakable by bark and by the bur litter under it.
- Oak foliage is uniformly orange-red, so oaks merge with the maples; the brief wants "more yellows and fewer reds".
- Yellow shrubs (spicebush, witch hazel) are the dominant shrub colour on every site.

### Distribution

- **Right:** doghobble arching at the stream; mossy boulders at the cascade; ferns denser near water; chestnut burs in the litter across the slopes.
- **Stream and ravine:** no rhododendron thicket in `ravine.png`, `stream.png` or `cascade.png`. The rhododendron portrait shows a single shrub beside the trail. No yellow birch identifiable by its bark near the water.
- **Dry ridge (`ridge.png`):** bare litter with scattered red saplings and yellow shrubs. No mountain laurel, no blueberry carpet, no galax, no bracken colony, no sedge lawn. The big ridge trunk is as large and flared as the cove giants; ridge trees should be smaller and more crooked.
- **Cove:** I could not pick out tulip poplar, basswood or buckeye in the cove views.
- **Openness:** sight lines of 30–50 m are about right for the brief's fire-maintained open understory. Shrub cover looks like 20–30%, within the stated range.
- **Forest floor cover, estimated from `floor.png` and the ground portraits:** litter and burs about 80% (target 55%), ferns about 8% (15%), moss and low evergreens 2–3% away from boulders (10%), grasses and sedges 2–3% (8%), other herbs about 3% (7%), rock and wood 2–3% away from the stream (5%). The floor is too much plain litter.

### Season (mid-October)

- **Right:** golden-amber chestnut with some green-centred leaves; burs on the ground, some open with nuts; fresh mixed litter; yellow tulip poplar and birch; scarlet black gum and dogwood; yellowed hay-scented and New York fern; bronze bracken; evergreen Christmas and wood fern; senescent trillium; evergreen rhododendron with the odd yellow leaf.
- **Wrong:** every deciduous tree carries a full crown at full colour. In mid-October buckeye and ash are nearly bare, tulip poplar and basswood half bare, and oaks and beech still part green. There is no leftover green in the canopy at all; Richards' painting is full of green-gold.
- **Not visible in the world:** witch hazel flowers, dogwood fruit, sourwood capsules, spicebush and partridgeberry berries, aster flowers or seed heads. The sprites for most of these exist on the sheets.
- **Burs:** good close up. From 3 m away they are small black dots. Fresh burs are straw-yellow to tan and 5–7 cm across.
- **Litter:** all leaves are flat, whole and brightly coloured. Add last year's brown and grey broken leaves beneath, plus twigs, acorns and hickory husks.

### Implausible, missing or out of scale

- Bark block size on dogwood and other thin stems is several times too large (snakeskin effect); the fallen log in the gap has bark stretched sideways.
- Ferns beyond about 10 m become rosettes of solid strap-shaped blades, like hostas or bromeliads (`october.png`, `stream.png`, `tree_serviceberry.png`).
- Herb leaves carry a mint or teal cast not found in these plants.
- A burlap-like crosshatch covers litter and foliage in most shots. It is a rendering matter, but it defeats leaf detail.
- Distant hillsides are covered in a scale-like pebble pattern rather than tree crowns.
- Missing from the brief's picture: big lichen-covered boulders away from the stream, vines on trunks, the massive forked leaning chestnut of the painting.

## 4. Ranked corrections (top 20)

1. **Rebuild trunk bases and taper.** Remove the cone skirt. At half the tree's height the trunk should still be at least 75% as thick as it is at breast height (1.4 m) for chestnut, tulip poplar and oaks. Limit root flare to the bottom 0.6–1.0 m on trees under 1 m thick and the bottom 1.5–2 m on giants; widen the diameter at ground by only 1.3–1.6×; break the flare into 4–7 separate rounded buttress roots (lobed cross-section, 15–30 cm deep) that run into the soil at different angles. Bury the junction under litter and moss so there is no visible seam. Give 1 chestnut giant in 5 a hollow or triangular fire scar 1–2 m tall at the base.

2. **Rebuild canopy crowns.** Clear bole for 50–60% of height (12–20 m). Then 3–6 main limbs, each 25–40% of the trunk's thickness, leaving at 30–50° from vertical at staggered heights at least 1–2 m apart, never as a single whorl. Crown width 12–20 m for canopy trees so that neighbours touch and the view from above shows 85–95% leaf cover outside the tree-fall gap. Chestnut: broad rounded heavy-limbed crown, 25–30 m tall. Tulip poplar: tallest (35–45 m), narrow high crown. White oak: wide near-horizontal gnarled limbs. Hemlock: cone.

3. **Fix hemlock colour, habit and numbers.** Foliage to deep green (#24452a, new growth #3d6b3a, underside pale grey-green); remove the blue-white highlight. Flat lacy sprays in horizontal tiers with drooping tips and a nodding leader. Within 30 m of water and on north-facing ravine walls let hemlock be 30–40% of stems; elsewhere 2–3%, so the stand total is about 8%.

4. **Give bark real relief and correct scale.** Bark must show depth, not lines: chestnut ridges 3–6 cm wide with 2–4 cm furrows on trunks over 80 cm, grey-brown (#6b6157 to #7d7368). Scale pattern to stem thickness: stems under 15 cm are smooth with lenticels; dogwood blocks 1–2 cm; black gum blocks 3–5 cm. Do not stretch bark along fallen logs. White oak: pale loose plates. Yellow birch: silver-bronze with papery horizontal curls.

5. **Put rhododendron where it belongs.** Dense continuous thickets 2–5 m tall within 15–25 m of the stream and in the ravine floor, covering 40–70% of that strip; none more than about 40 m from water. Doghobble along the bank edge beneath it. Remove the bold white side veins from rhododendron, laurel, doghobble and wintergreen leaves (keep a pale midrib and add gloss).

6. **Build the dry-ridge community.** Mountain laurel thickets 1.5–3 m tall at 30–50% cover; blueberry colonies 20–50 cm tall and 2–6 m across at 20–40% ground cover, crimson; galax and wintergreen colonies; bracken colonies; sedge lawns; hair-grass on rocks. Canopy of chestnut oak, chestnut, scarlet oak, black gum and sourwood at 18–25 m with crooked, thinner (40–80 cm) trunks. No spicebush, hobblebush, basswood, buckeye or tulip poplar here.

7. **Add old-growth dead wood and micro-relief.** Roughly 5–10 standing snags per hectare over 50 cm thick, tops broken at 5–20 m, bark sloughing, bracket fungi. 20–40 fallen logs per hectare, 10–30 m long and 0.4–1.5 m thick, in three states: fresh with bark, moss-covered, and collapsed red-brown rot with ferns and seedlings growing on them. Tip-up mounds: a recent one with an upright root plate 2–4 m across and a pit 0.5–1 m deep; old ones as paired 0.5–1 m humps and hollows every 15–30 m.

8. **Make the gap a gap.** A 20–30 m opening with the fallen trunk attached to its root plate and pit, a tangle of broken crown, and a thicket of saplings 2–6 m tall (tulip poplar, black birch, red maple, chestnut sprouts) plus bottlebrush grass, asters and hay-scented fern in full light.

9. **Fix fern distance rendering and growth pattern.** Keep cut-out pinnate fronds to at least 25–30 m and fade them out; never swap to solid blades. Hay-scented and New York fern as carpets of single fronds 5–20 m across; bracken colonies on the ridge 0.6–1.2 m tall; Christmas fern as scattered clumps on slopes; wood fern on rocks and logs; lady fern in moist flats. Raise fern cover to about 15%.

10. **Stagger the season by species.** Suggested share of leaves still on the tree: buckeye 10–20%; white ash 20–40%; tulip poplar and basswood 40–60%; black gum 50%; birches 60%; red maple 70%; chestnut 80%; sugar maple and hickory 85%; beech and all oaks 95%. Suggested green share among remaining leaves: chestnut 10–20%; beech 30%; red and white oak 30–40%; chestnut oak 30%; scarlet oak 60%.

11. **Correct the herb layer's colour and posture.** Remove the mint/teal cast: wild ginger matte #4f7a35 fading to #b9b24a; partridgeberry #1f4a2a with a cream midrib; rattlesnake plantain #2f5a44 with a white net; galax glossy #2e5d2e bronzing to #6d2a3a. Lift leaves off the ground on their stalks, cup and tilt them, and group them in colonies. Rebuild wintergreen as upright 5–12 cm sprigs with red berries. Raise low evergreens and moss to about 10% of the floor and other herbs to about 7%.

12. **Redraw the white oak leaf** as specified in the species table (7–9 finger lobes, deep sinuses, obovate, no waist) and give it the autumn mix of dull green, russet and wine.

13. **Give Fraser magnolia its own leaf and habit** (25–45 cm, eared base, umbrella whorls of 5–8 at branch tips, pale yellow to tan, slender often multi-stemmed tree 8–12 m). Keep the present sprite for cucumber magnolia.

14. **Redraw basswood and tulip poplar leaves** as specified in the species table (basswood: pointed tip, lopsided heart base; tulip poplar: four pointed lobes, notched tip, as wide as long).

15. **Reshape the understory trees.** No dense balls on poles. Dogwood: 4–8 m, leaning, flat tiers, red fruit clusters. Sourwood: curved trunk, drooping tips, tan capsule sprays. Hornbeam: 4–9 m, crooked fluted blue-grey trunk, flat crown. Serviceberry: 2–4 slender stems, open crown. Witch hazel: open vase, half bare, yellow ribbon flowers along twigs. Striped maple: green white-striped stem.

16. **Rebuild bracken** as a three-part horizontal blade on a stiff 40–80 cm stalk, as specified in the species table.

17. **Rebuild sedges and grasses and triple their cover (to about 8%).** Pennsylvania sedge lawns on dry slopes; plantain-leaved sedge on moist cove slopes; Appalachian sedge fountains; bottlebrush grass 80–130 cm with a visible bristly spike in gaps and along the trail; Bosc's panic grass 30–60 cm with broad short leaves; hair-grass on the ridge. Dimensions in the species table.

18. **Make white wood aster visible.** Colonies of 10–50 stems 30–70 cm tall on cove slopes and trail edges, dark zig-zag stems, heart-shaped toothed leaves 5–10 cm, flat-topped clusters of 1.5–2.5 cm heads with 6–10 white rays and a yellow-to-purple centre; half the heads already tan fluff. About 3–5% cover where present.

19. **Fix chestnut burs at distance and litter depth.** Burs 5–7 cm across including 1–2 cm spines, straw-yellow to tan-brown (#b59a55 to #8a6a3a), never black; 60–70% split into four valves with a pale tan lining; 5–15 per m² under chestnut crowns and almost none beyond 1.5 crown radii; leave a few on branch tips. Under the fresh leaves add a brown-grey layer of broken old leaves, twigs, acorns and hickory husks.

20. **Tune composition by site and finish the small leaf fixes.** Chestnut 40–50% of canopy on mid and upper slopes and 20–25% in the cove bottom; tulip poplar, sugar maple, basswood, buckeye, beech and magnolias in the cove bottom and lower slopes; spicebush within about 50 m of the stream; witch hazel on mid slopes; mapleleaf viburnum under oak and chestnut on drier slopes; hobblebush only in the cool ravine. Small leaf fixes: red oak bristle-tipped lobes; sugar maple wider lobes; witch hazel lopsided base; serviceberry rounded base; birch rounded or heart base; mayapple as a centred umbrella; rattlesnake plantain full white net. Add scarlet Virginia creeper climbing some trunks, and patchy moss and pale lichen in place of the uniform green band.

VERDICT: NOT SATISFIED — I would be satisfied when a botanist walking the world could name every listed species from its in-world leaf, bark and habit, and could tell cove, streamside ravine and dry ridge apart by their plants, in a forest with columnar buttressed boles, a closed heavy-limbed canopy, snags, logs and tip-up mounds, and a mid-October mix of bare, turning and still-green crowns.
