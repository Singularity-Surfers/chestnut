> "I want to make something like a procedurally generated photorealistic game world making the environment described in the conversation I imported."
> "Goal should be a photorealistic, procedurally generated environment of an old growth chestnut forest in the fall in full fall colors, with all the plant species described. With photorealistic groundcover, mossy rocks, realistic terrain/hills/streams/boulders and beautiful old growth chestnuts, maples, all the plant life described."
> "I'm trying to create an immersive environment to see and feel what that lost ecosystem would have looked like. Should have realistic lighting, time of day, vary the weather, goal is basically just beautiful, fully realistic plant life that would please a botanist, correct species and distributions of species as described, focus on making the details good like leaves, bark, moss, lighting, natural feeling."
> "The best general reference image for the 'vibe' is probably the Richards painting + the massive chestnut trees, though I'm wanting photorealism rather than stylistic."
> "most important goal is to realize a photorealistic old growth biodiverse chestnut forest in fall. (Make sure there's lots of procedural generation on all the plant varieties.)"

It is a real-time three.js/WebGL world that runs in a browser (first-person walk + fly), entirely procedural: no photo textures or scanned assets.

## The setting (from the owner's research notes)

Southern Appalachian cove, mid-October, c. 1850, before the chestnut blight. A moist, well-drained mountainside running down into a sheltered valley with a stream.

Illustrative canopy cover the owner wants: American chestnut 35%, oaks 20%, red maple 8%, sugar maple 4%, tulip poplar 10%, eastern hemlock 8%, birches 5%, American beech 4%, hickories 3%, other trees 3% (basswood, yellow buckeye, black gum, magnolias, ash).

Autumn colour: chestnuts golden yellow / amber / bronze (turning early, holding brown leaves late); red maple scarlet–crimson–orange; sugar maple yellow–orange–red; the forest had "more yellows and fewer reds than modern forests" — "an immense golden canopy punctuated by islands of scarlet and orange". Chestnut leaves 4–9 in long with sharply serrated edges. Chestnuts 80–100 ft tall, trunks "deeply furrowed gray-brown columns, some 5–7 feet in diameter".

Canopy companions named: northern red oak, chestnut oak, tulip poplar, American beech, eastern hemlock, yellow birch.
Understory trees (10–40 ft): flowering dogwood (scarlet), striped maple (yellow), sourwood (deep red), American hornbeam (orange/yellow), downy serviceberry (orange/red), Fraser magnolia (large yellowing leaves).
Shrubs: American witch hazel (10–25 ft, golden yellow, flowers in autumn), spicebush (6–12 ft, bright yellow, red berries, moist sites), mapleleaf viburnum (3–6 ft, pink/red/purple), hillside blueberry (1–3 ft, red, drier slopes), great rhododendron (5–20 ft, evergreen, along streams and ravines), mountain laurel (3–15 ft, evergreen, acidic slopes), mountain doghobble (2–6 ft, arching evergreen, moist ravines), hobblebush (3–10 ft, large round leaves turning wine-red, cooler forests).
Ferns: Christmas fern (evergreen), evergreen wood fern, southern lady fern (fine, feathery), hay-scented fern (yellowing), bracken (bronze), New York fern (low spreading colonies).
Grasses and sedges (in patches, especially where light reaches the ground): Pennsylvania sedge, Appalachian woodland sedge, plantain-leaved sedge, bottlebrush grass, Bosc's panic grass, wavy hair-grass.
Other ground cover: galax (glossy round leaves turning burgundy), wintergreen (evergreen, red berries), partridgeberry (creeping evergreen mats), striped wintergreen (dark leaves with white stripes), rattlesnake plantain (patterned evergreen leaves), wild ginger (heart-shaped leaves), white wood aster (small white late flowers), foamflower (low lobed leaves), trilliums and mayapple (mostly died back by October), Indian cucumber-root (whorled leaves), Virginia creeper (scarlet climbing vine).

Forest floor (illustrative surface cover): leaf litter and chestnut burs 55%, ferns 15%, mosses and low evergreen plants 10%, grasses and sedges 8%, other herbs 7%, exposed rocks and fallen wood 5%. Shrubs 15–30% cover in a moderately open stand, more near streams.

"An important historical difference": pre-blight forests had more open understories and more herbaceous vegetation; frequent low-intensity fire kept rhododendron thickets confined near streams. Richards' relatively open forest interior with patches of grasses, ferns, mosses and scattered shrubs is plausible.

The feel: "a vertically layered forest: enormous golden chestnuts and oaks overhead, red and orange maples and dogwoods beneath them, scattered evergreen hemlocks, colorful shrubs, and a largely golden-brown forest floor punctuated by green ferns, sedges and moss-covered boulders." In Richards' "October" (1863) an ecologist identified the massive tree at right as American chestnut and the slender leaning red tree in front of it as flowering dogwood.
